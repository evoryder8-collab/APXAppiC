# APEX Movement Science Ledger

**Applies to:** deterministic Session Briefing education and coaching cues
**First reviewed:** 2026-08-28
**Next evidence review:** 2027-02-28, or earlier when a shipped claim changes
**Scope:** generally healthy adults unless an entry states otherwise

This ledger records what APEX tells a user, why the app says it, how certain the claim is, and where the boundary lies. It is an education ledger, not a diagnosis or treatment protocol. A caution in the app tells the user when not to force a movement and when qualified assessment is appropriate; it never identifies an injured tissue.

## 1. Static stretching changes available range, but the mechanism is not “instant muscle lengthening”

**APEX application:** The mobility briefing says that range available today can change through tolerance and stiffness, while durable usable range needs repeated exposure and control. It does not say that one stretch permanently lengthens a muscle.

**Intended population:** Adults performing ordinary warm-up, cool-down, or flexibility work. Not a contracture-treatment protocol.

**Evidence:**

- [Mechanisms underlying range-of-motion improvements following acute and chronic static stretching](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40180774/) reports reduced stiffness after acute and chronic stretching, with increased stretch tolerance observed in chronic work; structural results vary by dose and study design.
- [Optimising the dose of static stretching to improve flexibility](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39614059/) found moderate acute and large chronic effects across 189 studies, with substantial heterogeneity in protocols and outcomes.
- [Static stretching and muscle architecture meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37318696/) found small or trivial architecture changes overall, with larger changes concentrated in higher-volume or higher-intensity protocols.

**Confidence:** High that static stretching can acutely and chronically increase measured range; moderate about the relative mechanism in an individual.

**Limitation:** Range-of-motion tests combine mechanical properties, tolerance, task familiarity, measurement position, and neural state. APEX must not assign one mechanism to one user from the sensation alone.

## 2. Static, dynamic, PNF, and loaded end-range work are different tools

**APEX application:** The briefing distinguishes the four methods. It gives roughly 30–60 seconds as a practical range for a prescribed static hold and places dynamic repetitions in the warm-up frame. It does not present 30–60 seconds as a universal optimum or a reason to ignore the actual prescription.

**Intended population:** Healthy adult trainees. Condition-specific rehabilitation needs an individual prescription.

**Evidence:**

- [Acute effects of muscle stretching on physical performance, range of motion, and injury incidence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26642915/) reviews static, dynamic, and PNF methods and shows that method, duration, and the subsequent task matter.
- [Optimising the dose of static stretching](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39614059/) supports a dose-response ceiling at the session and weekly level, not one magic hold duration.
- [Strength training versus stretching for improving range of motion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33917036/) found no clear between-protocol range advantage in the available heterogeneous trials.
- [Resistance training improves range of motion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36622555/) supports loaded movement as a legitimate range-of-motion tool rather than treating passive stretching as the only route.

**Confidence:** Moderate.

**Limitation:** “Loaded end range” covers many exercises, loads, and intent levels. The copy teaches the distinction; it does not prescribe load or diagnose why range is limited.

## 3. Usable range also needs strength and control

**APEX application:** APEX tells users with mobility work—and especially an optional hypermobility marker—to stay short of an uncontrolled passive end position and train control there.

**Intended population:** General trainees, including people who report that they already have abundant range. This is not a hypermobility-spectrum-disorder diagnosis.

**Evidence:**

- [Strength training versus stretching for improving range of motion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33917036/) found strength training and stretching capable of improving range, without a clear difference between them in the pooled comparisons.
- [Resistance training improves range of motion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36622555/) supports full or targeted range resistance work as a flexibility intervention.

**Confidence:** Moderate for the general claim; low for selecting an individual end-range dose without assessment.

**Limitation:** A persisted hypermobility marker changes conservative wording only. APEX does not infer hypermobility from exercise performance, and roadmap item 7.1 owns any future baseline assessment.

## 4. A pinch, hard block, or neural symptom is not a cue to force more range

**APEX application:** The briefing distinguishes a broad muscular stretch sensation from pinching, a hard joint block, tingling, burning, numbness, or an electric line. It tells the user to back off and seek assessment if the symptom persists.

**Intended population:** General trainees using mobility or yoga movements.

**Evidence:**

- [Geometry and function of the ankle joint complex](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20300732/) describes articular geometry and joint surfaces as determinants of kinematics and maximal range.
- [Static plantar-flexor stiffness in chronic ankle instability](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37640966/) found weight-bearing dorsiflexion limitation that was largely attributable to factors other than increased plantar-flexor muscle stiffness in that small study.
- [Neural mobilisation in sport](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39593463/) found limited and scarce evidence for neural testing and mobilisation despite possible range and perceived-tension effects.

**Confidence:** High for the stop/back-off safety boundary; low for identifying which structure causes an individual sensation.

**Limitation:** Sensation words do not diagnose a nerve, capsule, bone, muscle, or injury. The app deliberately recommends assessment rather than naming tissue.

## 5. Productive resistance training does not require every set to reach momentary failure

**APEX application:** Strength/bodyweight copy prioritises repeatable positions and says a grinding repetition is not required for every useful set. Load, leverage, range, and tempo are presented as ways to progress an exercise.

**Intended population:** Adult recreational and trained lifters. The briefing follows the installed prescription rather than replacing its RIR target.

**Evidence:**

- [Failure versus non-failure resistance training meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/) found no evidence that momentary failure is categorically superior for hypertrophy when comparisons and volume are considered.
- [Proximity to failure, strength gain, and hypertrophy meta-regressions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/) suggests hypertrophy may trend upward closer to failure while strength adaptations occur across a wider RIR range; estimates and study methods remain uncertain.

**Confidence:** High that every set need not reach failure; moderate about the ideal RIR for an individual exercise and goal.

**Limitation:** The app does not claim all efforts are equivalent. Exercise selection, load, volume, training status, safety, and the prescribed goal still matter.

## 6. Isometric strength is task- and angle-specific

**APEX application:** The isometric briefing says adaptation is strongest around the held angle and task, and ends the hold when position or ordinary breathing fails.

**Intended population:** Healthy adults performing voluntary isometric resistance exercise.

**Evidence:**

- [Is the joint-angle specificity of isometric resistance training real?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31522276/) found robust angle-specific strength gains in a controlled training study, with weaker evidence about the neural mechanism.
- [Task specificity of isometric resistance training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40314751/) synthesises transfer across testing and training tasks and supports cautious specificity wording.

**Confidence:** Moderate to high.

**Limitation:** Adaptation is not confined to one exact degree, and transfer depends on muscle length, task, testing mode, and programme design. APEX says “around,” not “only at.”

## 7. Continuous cardio and intervals are distinct aerobic tools; recovery is part of an interval dose

**APEX application:** Continuous cardio is framed as purposeful sustainable work. Interval copy treats recovery as part of the prescription and asks for an opening pace that preserves later-bout quality.

**Intended population:** Healthy trained and untrained adults; not an exercise-clearance protocol for cardiovascular disease.

**Evidence:**

- [Physiological adaptations to interval training and the role of exercise intensity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27748956/) describes adaptations across interval and moderate continuous training, with intensity, duration, and frequency interactions still incompletely resolved.
- [Active versus passive recovery in long-term interval training](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38443585/) found improvements under both recovery modes and insufficient evidence that one mode is universally superior.
- [HIIT and cardiorespiratory fitness umbrella review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38760916/) synthesised 24 systematic reviews while noting that many underlying reviews had moderate-to-critically-low methodological quality.

**Confidence:** High that both continuous and interval formats can train aerobic fitness; moderate for the exact optimal work/recovery pattern.

**Limitation:** “Later bouts look like the first” is a practical quality cue, not a physiological threshold or a requirement for identical pace.

## 8. Recovery work may change short-term range or soreness; it does not break fascia or erase training stress

**APEX application:** The recovery briefing uses modest sensation/range language and explicitly rejects fascia-breaking and training-stress-erasure claims.

**Intended population:** Healthy adults using foam rolling or easy movement for recovery.

**Evidence:**

- [Foam rolling training effects on performance and recovery](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32825976/) supports small acute range and perceived-recovery effects while finding limited evidence for broad performance claims.
- [Acute foam rolling and range of motion meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31628662/) supports short-term range changes without establishing “fascia breaking” as the mechanism.
- [Chronic foam rolling and range of motion meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35616852/) found range effects with mechanisms and protocols still uncertain.

**Confidence:** Moderate for short-term range/perceived effects; high that “breaking fascia” is not an evidence-based user explanation.

**Limitation:** Perceived soreness, measured range, and recovery of performance are different outcomes. APEX does not collapse them into one recovery score.

## 9. Lactate is not the cause of next-day muscle soreness

**APEX application:** Recovery copy says that clearing lactate does not prevent next-day soreness.

**Intended population:** General trainees after ordinary exercise.

**Evidence:**

- [Blood lactate is not related to delayed-onset muscle soreness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27409551/) found no relationship that supports lactate as the cause of DOMS.

**Confidence:** High.

**Limitation:** DOMS has multiple proposed contributors and is not a direct measure of workout quality, damage, or adaptation. The app makes no causal diagnosis for an individual’s pain.

## 10. Passive stretching alone is not an injury-prevention guarantee

**APEX application:** No Session Briefing tells a user that stretching alone prevents injury. Safety copy instead responds to symptoms, control, load, and condition-specific modification.

**Intended population:** General sporting and exercise populations represented in the reviews.

**Evidence:**

- [Stretching before or after exercise and risk of injury or soreness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15076777/) did not support clinically important reductions in post-exercise soreness and found insufficient evidence for a broad injury-prevention guarantee.
- [Effects of stretching before and after exercising on soreness and risk of injury](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15782063/) similarly found no significant association with total injury reduction across the included evidence.

**Confidence:** High that passive stretching alone should not be sold as a guarantee; low for reducing all injuries to one intervention rule.

**Limitation:** Injury risk depends on sport, exposure, load management, prior injury, strength, equipment, environment, and many other factors.

## 11. Yoga is generally practice, while real condition-specific modifications still matter

**APEX application:** Yoga copy prioritises controlled breath and stable positions. It advises qualified modification when pregnancy, glaucoma, fragile bones, heat, long supine holds, inversions, or forceful breath work may apply. It does not ban yoga for everyone with those conditions.

**Intended population:** General adult yoga participants. Pregnancy guidance assumes that individual obstetric or medical contraindications still require clinician input.

**Evidence:**

- [ACOG: Exercise During Pregnancy](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/exercise-during-pregnancy) supports modified yoga in uncomplicated pregnancy while advising against hot yoga and long motionless or supine positions.
- [ACOG Committee Opinion 804](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2020/04/physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period) states that exercise is generally desirable in uncomplicated pregnancy but medical or obstetric complications require individual evaluation; prolonged supine exercise after 20 weeks can reduce venous return.
- [Adverse events associated with yoga](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24146758/) is a case-report/series review, not an incidence study; it identifies extreme postures, forceful breathing, glaucoma, and compromised bone as reasons for careful adaptation.
- [Intraocular pressure during four common head-down yoga positions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26698309/) found rapid temporary IOP increases in small glaucoma and control samples.
- [Yoga safety meta-analysis of randomised trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26116216/) found yoga generally comparable in safety to usual care or exercise while noting incomplete adverse-event reporting.

**Confidence:** High for avoiding hot yoga and prolonged supine work in pregnancy and for acute IOP rise in head-down positions; moderate for broad condition-specific yoga rules because incidence and long-term-risk data are limited.

**Limitation:** Case reports cannot estimate frequency or prove that every listed practice harms every person. The app uses conditional language and qualified guidance, never universal prohibition or medical clearance.

## Shipped-language exclusions

Every locale must preserve these boundaries in meaning, even when its native sentence structure differs:

- Acute stretching is not described as permanently lengthening muscle.
- Foam rolling is not described as breaking fascia.
- Lactate is not described as causing next-day soreness.
- Passive stretching alone is not described as preventing injury.
- Pain, a hard block, or neural-type symptoms are not framed as targets to push through.
- Training to failure is not mandatory for every productive set.
- Yoga cautions remain conditional and direct the user to appropriate modification or qualified guidance.
